A digital nomad visa lets you live in another country while you keep working for your current employer or your own US clients. This post explains how these visas work, compares the main options, and helps you shortlist two or three countries.

What a digital nomad visa is

It's a residence permit that lets you live in another country while keeping your current job. Your income stays tied to somewhere else, so you aren't taking a local job. The country you move to is called the host country.

Example: You're an American software developer employed by a US company, and you work from home. You get a digital nomad visa from Spain, move to Valencia, and keep working for the same US company, which keeps paying you in US dollars. You live legally in Spain, but you don't work for a Spanish employer or take Spanish clients.

The same works if you're self-employed: you can live in Spain on this visa if your clients are in the US (or anywhere outside Spain).

What countries typically require

  • Income from outside the host country. In the example above, your paycheck must come from the US company, not a Spanish company. Many countries cap how much income can come from local clients (Spain, for instance, limits it to a minority share of your income).
  • Minimum monthly income. You must show you earn at least a set amount, such as about €2,849 per month for Spain.
  • Proof of employment. This is usually an employment contract or letter from your US employer, or contracts with your clients if you're self-employed.
  • Health insurance that is valid in the host country.
  • A clean criminal record, typically an FBI background check.
  • Some work history. Many countries want proof that you've already worked remotely, or at least a minimum contract length.

Taxes

  • US taxes still apply. In the example, you'd still file a US tax return every year even though you live in Spain, because Americans are taxed on worldwide income.
  • Spanish tax residency is a separate question. If you spend more than about 183 days a year in Spain, Spain may also treat you as a tax resident and want to tax you. Spain's visa includes a reduced flat 24% rate for the first four years, but you should confirm how that interacts with your US taxes.

Thailand: Destination Thailand Visa (DTV)

  • Who qualifies: Remote workers and freelancers with foreign employers or clients, people doing Thai "soft power" activities (Muay Thai, cooking courses, medical treatment), and spouses and children of holders.
  • Fee: THB 10,000.
  • Application: Online only, through Thailand's e-visa system, and from outside Thailand.
  • You must leave between stays: After each stay you have to exit the country before the next one.
  • Work restriction: It's a special tourist-type visa. You can't get a Thai work permit or work for Thai companies or Thai clients.
  • Recent change (August 31, 2026): A police clearance certificate is now mandatory for all applicants. A residence-document requirement was also changed, but the wording found refers to the London embassy's rules, so ask your Thai embassy or consulate what Americans need to submit.
  • Higher tier: The LTR "Work from Thailand Professional" track runs 10 years but requires about $80,000 per year in income.

Panama: Short-Stay Visa for Remote Workers

  • Requirements: International health insurance valid in Panama, an employment letter describing your role and income, and bank statements. The income must come entirely from outside Panama.
  • Fee: About $250 in government fees.
  • Application: One source says you submit it in Panama through a legal representative, and another says the promised online portal still isn't live. Check the official Panama immigration page (migracion.gob.pa) for the current process.
  • Work restriction: You can't work for a Panamanian employer or local clients.
  • Residency: It does not lead to permanent residency directly, but you can apply for a different residency visa separately.
  • Family: Sources disagree. One calls it a solo visa and another says dependents are typically covered, so confirm this if you're bringing family.

Vietnam: no digital nomad visa

  • What people actually use: A 90-day multiple-entry e-visa, about $50, open to all nationalities. It's technically a tourist status, and it generally can't be extended from inside Vietnam. Most nomads leave every three months (often to Cambodia, Laos or Thailand) and re-enter on a new visa.
  • Other routes: A new tech-talent visa (UĐ1/UĐ2) launched July 1, 2026 for skilled tech and digital professionals, and an investor visa for people who open a Vietnamese company (from roughly $120,000 in capital). A "Golden Visa" has been discussed but is still only a proposal.
  • Bottom line: It's cheap and flexible for rotating stays, but it's not a stable base like Thailand's DTV.

Singapore: no digital nomad visa

  • Tourist entry: Americans get 30 to 90 days visa-free, but working is prohibited on a tourist entry. One source says this includes remote work for overseas employers. Others describe a "work anywhere" gray area, so don't rely on it.
  • ONE Pass (Overseas Networks & Expertise): For top talent only. It requires S$30,000 per month (about $22,000) and lasts 5 years. It is not a typical nomad route.
  • EntrePass: For founders starting an innovative Singapore-incorporated company.
  • Employment Pass: Requires a job offer from a Singapore employer.
  • Bottom line: Singapore is realistic only as a short visit or a base for high earners and founders.

Estonia: Digital Nomad Visa

  • Type and duration: A Type D long-stay visa, valid for up to 12 months. Sources disagree on whether it can be extended, so check. It does not lead directly to permanent residency.
  • Income requirement: Sources conflict. Recent guidance says €4,500 gross per month from foreign sources over the prior six months. The official long-stay visa table read in July 2026 shows €3,960 per month (€132 per day), with income evidence for the prior three months. Confirm the current figure with the Estonian authorities.
  • Fee: Reported as €120 by one source and lower by others, so expect "around €100" and check the official page.
  • Other requirements: Valid passport, health insurance, a clean criminal record, and an accommodation plan.
  • Application: You can prefill the form online, but the process is not completed remotely.
  • Quota: It is a separate long-stay category and does not count against Estonia's annual immigration quota.
  • Work restriction: It covers remote work for employers or clients outside Estonia.
  • Taxes: Foreign-sourced remote income is typically not taxed in Estonia while you remain a non-resident for tax purposes. A tax resident pays a 20% flat rate.
  • Family: Sources disagree, with one saying each family member needs a separate application and another saying dependents aren't allowed, so confirm.
  • Don't confuse with e-Residency: E-Residency is a separate program for running a business online and does not give you the right to live in Estonia.

How they differ

  • Cost of getting in. Thailand (funds only), Brazil, Italy and Spain have the lowest bars. Estonia, Portugal, Japan and Singapore are the most demanding.
  • Tax treatment. Croatia, Costa Rica, Panama and Barbados don't tax foreign income, and Spain gives a reduced rate for four years. The rest tax you as a resident once you're there longer than about 183 days a year.
  • Path to staying permanently. Spain and Portugal are the strongest, since their time counts toward permanent residency. Most of the others are temporary permits you can renew.
  • Bringing family. Spain and Portugal allow dependents, with higher income requirements. Thailand covers spouses and children. Other countries often require extra income or savings per person.
  • Distance and language. Costa Rica, Panama and Barbados are a short flight with minimal time difference. Europe gives easy travel within the Schengen Area but needs more planning. Thailand and Vietnam are far from the US.

Picks for US citizens

  1. Spain: best balance of income bar, taxes, residency path and lifestyle.
  2. Portugal: best if you want a large English-speaking expat community and can meet the higher income.
  3. Thailand (DTV): best long-stay option in Asia, with a low financial bar and a 5-year visa.
  4. Panama or Costa Rica: best if you want to stay close to the US with simple logistics and no local tax on foreign income.
  5. Croatia: best if saving on local income tax matters most.
  6. Italy or Greece: best if your income is lower or you want an easier application.
  7. Vietnam: best for cheap rotating stays, if you're fine with visa runs.

US taxes still apply in every case, since Americans are taxed on worldwide income wherever they live.
