On a recent trip through Poland, Turkey and Moldova, I paid with cards, transfers, cash and, a few times, not at all. Below is what I saw, written up the way I'd report it from the field.

My setup: Wise, and almost nothing else

I use Wise, and for most trips it is the best tool I have found. Three things make it work for me:

  • The exchange rate. Wise converts at the mid-market rate and shows its fee up front. No hidden markup buried in the rate, which is where banks quietly take their cut.
  • Real local account details. Wise gives you actual account numbers in a set of currencies: a US routing number, a UK sort code, a euro IBAN, and others including Turkish lira. People can pay you as if you were a local, and you can move money cheaply between them.
  • One card, many balances. I top up the currency I need before I land, and the card spends from that balance. No airport kiosk, no surprise conversion at the terminal.
  • The receipt lives with the payment. This is the feature I love most. I snap a photo of the receipt and attach it right to the transaction. Weeks later, when I'm reconciling a trip, the proof is already there. No shoebox of crumpled paper.

In short, Wise solves the bank side of travel money very well. What it cannot solve is the street side: what a small vendor is actually willing to accept.

Poland and Turkey: the easy part

In Poland, paying was a non-event. I held złoty in Wise, and the card was accepted almost everywhere I went: shops, cafes, transit. Contactless is so normal there that cash felt optional.

Turkey was similar. Card terminals are everywhere, and Wise even offers lira account details. Prices move fast with the lira, so converting just before spending rather than weeks ahead made a real difference.

After Poland and Turkey, it's easy to believe that paying abroad is no longer a problem: bring a good card and you're set. Moldova proved me wrong.

Moldova: where my money stopped working

Moldova was a different story. In Chișinău's larger stores, my card worked. But the places that make a trip worth taking, the small country shops, the street food stands, public transportation, all said the same thing: cash only, no cards.

My field notes, by type of barrier:

  1. Acceptance barrier. Small vendors took cash only. Not because they disliked cards, but because a terminal costs money and fees they don't need for local customers.
  2. Rail barrier. Wise handles more than 40 currencies, yet it simply doesn't support the Moldovan leu. When a company that good at currencies leaves one out, it points to a structural problem behind it: strict regulation, a lack of local banking partners, or a market too small to justify the effort.
  3. Access barrier. Moldova has a modern national instant-payment system, MIA, with QR codes at the till. Locals pay in seconds for free. Visitors can't join, because it runs through Moldovan bank apps. The digital rail existed; I just had no on-ramp.
  4. No trust problem at all. The vendors were friendly and wanted my business. When I offered to pay by phone, they were happy to try. They just had no way to accept it.

The old advice is to find an ATM. That advice is out of date. Like a lot of people today, I don't carry a debit card at all. I pay with my phone and a credit card, and I haven't typed a PIN in a decade. For me, an ATM isn't a fallback; it's a dead end, with withdrawal fees and conversion markups on top.

The barrier that mattered most was the third one. The merchant was willing, the payment rail was there. Only I couldn't get on it.

Taxis and the no-internet problem

If there is one payment every traveler makes, it's the taxi. Every trip starts and ends with one: airport to hotel, train station to old town, back to the airport at 5 a.m. Shops and restaurants are optional. The taxi isn't.

That makes the taxi the moment that decides how a country feels. If I land and can't pay a driver without hunting for cash, the whole destination starts on the wrong foot. When I'm choosing between two countries for a trip, "can I get around easily?" quietly tips the scale.

And taxis come with a unique problem of their own: no internet. Think about when you actually take one:

  • Right after landing. No local SIM yet, roaming not working, airport Wi-Fi behind a login page.
  • Between cities. Long stretches of road with weak or no coverage.
  • In the countryside. Villages, mountains, and border areas where the signal simply drops.
  • The driver's side, too. An older phone, a cheap data plan, or no data left by the end of the month.

Every QR payment app I know assumes both phones are online at the moment of payment. In a taxi, that assumption breaks exactly when you need it most.

Offline payments

This is where crypto technology has something real to offer. A crypto payment is, at its core, a digitally signed message. My phone can create and sign it without any connection, and anyone can later verify that it really came from me. Paying offline could work like this:

  1. Set aside a travel float. While I still have internet, the app locks a small amount, say enough for a few rides, so it can't be spent twice.
  2. Pay phone to phone. At the end of the ride, my phone signs a payment for the fare and hands it to the driver's phone by QR code, NFC or Bluetooth. No internet needed on either side.
  3. Settle when anyone reconnects. Whichever phone gets a signal first submits the payment. The driver is paid in local currency, and my float is reduced.

The driver doesn't have to trust me, because the money was locked in advance and the signature proves it's mine. I don't have to find cash. And the ride ends the way it should: a quick tap, a thank you, and out the door.

Offline payment is the feature that would turn a nice travel app into one I couldn't travel without.

The lesson: the last meter is local

Some places are harder still. In Russia, foreign Visa and Mastercard cards stopped working in 2022, so a visitor is back to carrying cash from home.

Moldova showed me that a fair exchange rate is no longer the hard part. Wherever my card was accepted, Wise gave me a good rate. The gap is the last meter: the local QR code taped to the counter, built only for people with a local bank account.

Closing that gap one traveler at a time doesn't work in countries like Moldova, where cross-border transfers are tightly regulated. Every tourist would have to push their own small international transfer through bank checks and paperwork, just to buy lunch.

A better design flips it around. The traveler pays one licensed operator in their home currency. The operator nets thousands of small payments together and settles them in one go with a licensed local partner, using stablecoins as a fast, cheap settlement rail between them. The partner pays each merchant in lei through the national QR system. One regulated entity handles compliance once, instead of every customer facing it alone. The merchant just sees a normal local payment.

The app someone should build

Paying by local QR solves the moment at the counter. But for anyone traveling for work, the pain comes later: turning a week of coffees, taxis and hotel nights into an expense report the company will actually reimburse.

Someone should build a travel payment app where every QR payment carries its own story. You scan, you pay, and the transaction is born with rich metadata attached:

Data on each paymentWhere it comes from
Amount, currency, rate, feeThe payment itself
Merchant name and IDThe merchant's QR code
Location and timeThe phone
Expense type (transportation, lodging, meals, groceries, supplies)AI, from the merchant and the receipt
Receipt photo and line itemsCamera; AI reads the text
Company, client or projectOne tap, or remembered per trip
Business or personalOne tap

Then the app does the boring part for you:

  • Reports by date. A day-by-day summary of the trip, with daily totals.
  • Totals by category. Transportation, lodging, groceries, meals and so on, in both local currency and your home currency.
  • A reimbursement invoice. One click produces an invoice for the company, with every receipt attached and every line tied to a real payment.

The key ingredient is AI. Travel data is messy: a blurry receipt in Vietnamese, a QR code with a cryptic merchant name, a note typed in a hurry. The app should read all of that unstructured information and turn it into clean, structured records: the right category, the real business name, the tax amount pulled off the receipt. By the end of the trip, the data is ready for an accountant, not a weekend of cleanup.

That changes who the product is for. A backpacker saves a few dollars on ATM fees. A business traveler, a consultant, or a remote team saves hours on every trip, and a company could roll the app out to all its traveling staff.

Reviews that can't be faked

There's a second gift hidden in all that payment data: honest recommendations.

Star ratings are easy to game. A restaurant asks friends for five stars, or a competitor posts one-star attacks. But payments don't lie. If the app can see that a noodle shop has many repeat customers, people who come back again and again and pay each time, that's the strongest signal there is. Nobody returns five times to a place they didn't like. And the traveler never has to write a single review for that signal to exist.

When people do leave feedback, the app can weigh it by money actually spent, not by headcount. A rating from someone who ate there eight times counts for more than one from someone who never paid at all. Fake reviews from people who were never customers simply carry no weight.

For a traveler, that's the question that matters most in a new city: where do the regulars actually go?

Before you rely on your phone in a new city

Five questions tell you how much cash you'll really need:

  1. What do the small places take? Street food, markets, kiosks: cash, card, or the local QR code?
  2. How do taxis get paid? Cash, a card in the car, or only through a ride app?
  3. Will I have internet the moment I land? An eSIM ready to go, or hunting for airport Wi-Fi?
  4. Is there a local QR system, and can a visitor use it? Some countries let foreign apps in; others don't.
  5. What do locals do when payment fails? Their workaround is usually the one to copy.

If most answers point to cash, carry some. If they point to QR, set up an app that can pay those codes before you fly.

Until travel apps close that last meter, my rule for any new country stays simple: check the local payment app before you pack a wallet.
